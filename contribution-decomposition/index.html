PBS/MPS Attribution Framework

What Is Contribution Decomposition?

Contribution Decomposition is the proposed analytical framework within PBS/MPS for examining how multiple documented contributions relate to a measurable outcome and how attributable economic participation could be evaluated.

What problem does Contribution Decomposition address?

Valuable outcomes are often collaborative.

A research discovery may reflect work performed by many people across different stages of an investigation. An agricultural genetic asset may result from generations of breeding, crossing, selection, observation, and field testing. A creative work may build on numerous identifiable contributions. An artificial-intelligence system may incorporate information, judgments, corrections, examples, or other inputs originating from many different sources.

Recording that contributors participated is only the first problem. The harder question is how documented contributions relate to the resulting measurable outcome.

Contribution Decomposition is the part of PBS/MPS intended to examine that relationship.

Within the proposed architecture, the Provenance Ledger establishes the contribution record, the Performance Oracle supplies evidence about outcomes, and Contribution Decomposition examines the relationship between the two.

A record of participation is not yet a measure of contribution

Suppose ten people participate in producing a valuable result. A record showing that all ten participated may be accurate, but that record alone tells us very little about how the result was produced.

Equal participation cannot simply be assumed. One contribution may have been indispensable while another was easily substitutable. Several seemingly modest contributions may have become valuable only when combined. An early contribution may have shaped everything that followed, while a later contribution may have produced an immediate but short-lived improvement.

The opposite problem also exists. A highly visible contribution is not necessarily the most causally important one. The person closest to the final output may be easiest to identify, while earlier contributors provided knowledge, data, selections, corrections, or infrastructure on which the final contribution depended.

This is why PBS/MPS separates

documentation

from

attribution.

A provenance record can establish that an input existed, identify its source, place it in time, and record the context in which it entered a project. But provenance by itself does not establish the economic importance of that input. Likewise, performance evidence can establish that a system, asset, model, or process performed well, but performance by itself does not establish who deserves credit for the result. The later PBS/MPS research therefore describes the complete analytical sequence as:

Provenance → Performance → Attribution/Allocation → Entitlement/Payment

Contribution Decomposition occupies the bridge between evidence of participation and a proposed allocation of attributable value.

The central question is counterfactual

At its simplest, Contribution Decomposition asks a question resembling:

What difference did this contribution make?

The mathematical materials express this through the idea of marginal contribution. Given a set of contributions, the marginal contribution of one input can be considered as the difference between the value achieved with that contribution and the value associated with the relevant comparison in which it is absent.

That difficulty matters.

In an artificial-intelligence system, for example, information can become embedded within training data, model parameters, and resulting behaviors. Simply removing one contribution and rerunning the entire history may be impractical or may change the environment in ways that make the comparison misleading. The proposed framework therefore discusses approximate approaches, including perturbing, masking, or down-weighting an input and examining changes in relevant performance measures. These estimates have approximation error and depend on assumptions about the model being studied.

That qualification should remain central to the public explanation.

Contribution Decomposition is

not a machine for discovering a single metaphysically correct percentage of credit.

It is a proposed analytical framework for constructing and testing evidence about contribution.

Contributions can interact

The individual-contribution question becomes harder because contributions do not necessarily create value independently.

Two contributions may each have little value alone but become highly valuable together. A breeding decision may matter because it combines two lines with complementary traits. A scientific technique may become important only when paired with a particular dataset. One contributor may identify a problem while another supplies the method that resolves it.

PBS/MPS refers to this additional modeled value from contributions acting together as

synergy.

The public glossary defines Contribution Decomposition as estimating the marginal, interacting, and time-dependent value of contributions, while synergy is defined as additional modeled value associated with contributions operating together rather than separately.

This prevents a simplistic model in which every contributor is treated as an isolated producer. It also creates an important conceptual shift. Sometimes the valuable unit may not be a person at all. It may be a

team, coalition, sequence, or interaction.

The later PBS/MPS work explicitly acknowledges this. Where individual separation would be artificial, the framework contemplates team- or coalition-level allocation rather than forcing false individual precision. It identifies excessive sensitivity to arbitrary decomposition choices as a failure condition.

In practical terms, the right answer to “Which individual created this value?” may sometimes be:

The evidence does not support decomposing the result that far.

That is not a failure of measurement. Recognizing that limit may be a sign that the analysis is working responsibly.

Contributions also operate through time

Contribution is not only collaborative. It can be persistent.

A useful contribution may continue affecting outcomes long after the original contribution was made. Other contributions may become obsolete quickly. Some may disappear as systems change. Others may be incorporated into later contributions and continue indirectly. The mathematical model therefore introduces a

persistence function

to represent the modeled continuation of a contribution's influence through time. It also treats the relevant contribution set as dynamic, recognizing that the environment itself changes as new contributions arrive.

This matters especially for cumulative innovation.

Consider agricultural breeding. A present-day variety may contain a history of parent lines, crosses, selections, and subsequent performance observations. The SeedBid Claims Outline describes genetic-lineage records, breeding history, and measurable growth characteristics such as germination, disease resistance, drought tolerance, and yield-related indicators.

The attribution question is therefore not merely who worked on the final variety. It is whether earlier documented contributions remain materially connected to later performance, and if so, how strongly.

Contribution Decomposition is intended to make that persistence an explicit analytical question rather than silently assigning all credit to the most recent observable contributor.

Cooperative game theory provides one allocation framework

Where a well-defined value function can be constructed, PBS/MPS proposes using or approximating a Shapley-style allocation.

Where a well-defined value function can be constructed, PBS/MPS proposes using or approximating a Shapley-style allocation.

The attraction of this framework is not that the word “Shapley” magically makes an allocation correct. Rather, the classical Shapley value supplies a specific normative rule for cooperative allocation. Under a specified cooperative game, the exact Shapley allocation is characterized by the familiar requirements of efficiency, symmetry, the null-player property, and additivity. Contribution Decomposition formula expressly clarifies that PBS/MPS

does not introduce a new uniqueness theorem.

It proposes applying the classical result to provenance-documented contributions.

That distinction is important because there are actually two questions.

The first is:

Given this value function, how should its value be allocated under the chosen rule?

The second, and arguably harder question, is:

Did we specify the right value function in the first place?

The classical theorem can address the first question under its assumptions. It cannot prove that the empirical model, performance measure, counterfactual, dataset, or attribution method used to construct the value function correctly represents reality. The current mathematical paper makes this limitation explicit: the uniqueness result for the exact Shapley allocation does not make an estimated value function or computational approximation unique or exact.

That is the appropriate boundary around the claim.

Decomposition should expose uncertainty, not conceal it

Any serious attribution system confronts ambiguity.

What baseline should be used? Which outcome should count as performance? How should missing evidence be handled? What happens when two reasonable attribution models produce different results? How dependent is an allocation on a particular random seed, counterfactual assumption, or metric? These are not peripheral implementation details. They determine whether the resulting attribution is credible. For that reason, the current PBS/MPS research makes

uncertainty-aware attribution

one of the requirements of an adequate system. Confidence, approximation error, model sensitivity, counterfactual dependence, and unresolved causal ambiguity should be disclosed rather than collapsed into a deceptively precise number.

The standard of success is therefore not that one model prints a percentage.

A stronger result would show that an allocation remains reasonably stable across defensible models, counterfactuals, metrics, and random seeds. The proposed proof conditions specifically call for sensitivity analysis, confidence intervals, and measures of allocation and rank stability.

If reasonable assumptions produce radically different allocations, that instability is itself a finding. Contribution Decomposition should reveal it.

Some contributions may be negative or inseparable

A contribution system also should not begin with the assumption that every recorded contribution increased value. Some interventions may have no measurable effect. Some may reduce performance. Some may impose costs that outweigh their benefits. The later PBS/MPS framework therefore contemplates negative or null marginal contribution and warns against systems that produce systematically one-sided rewards. It also distinguishes attribution from legal liability: estimating that an input reduced a performance measure is not, by itself, a determination that a contributor is legally responsible for the resulting loss. Similarly, some collaborative outcomes may simply resist individual decomposition. A team's output may be genuinely inseparable. Tacit knowledge may not have been documented. Historical evidence may be incomplete. The relevant counterfactual may be impossible to reconstruct with acceptable confidence. In those circumstances, PBS/MPS should not manufacture precision because an allocation mechanism wants a number. The current research instead provides a domain-unsuitability rule: granular allocation should be rejected or narrowed where provenance is inadequate, meaningful performance cannot be observed, attribution is unstable across reasonable models, privacy costs are disproportionate, or administration consumes too much of the value being allocated.

Decomposition is not the same thing as entitlement

There is another essential boundary.

Even an excellent attribution estimate does not automatically create ownership.

The PBS/MPS working paper states the point directly:

a Shapley-style allocation is not an entitlement merely because it is mathematically principled.

A separate legal or contractual rule must determine whether attributed contribution produces a royalty, license right, revenue share, cooperative interest, security, statutory entitlement, or some other recognized economic arrangement.

This separates three concepts that can otherwise become confused:

1.

Attribution:

What relationship does the evidence support between a contribution and an outcome? 2.

Allocation:

Under a specified rule, how would measured value be distributed? 3.

Entitlement:

What does contract or law actually require anyone to receive?

Contribution Decomposition primarily addresses the first two.

The third belongs to institutional and legal design.

That distinction has practical importance. The DFI response that the contemplated PBS/MPS architecture reaches beyond securities law and raises broader issues including property and intellectual-property rights. The agency also emphasized that its response should not be treated as legal or financial advice or as general project approval.

The method must justify its own cost

There is finally an economic question about Contribution Decomposition itself:

Is measuring contribution worth doing?

Granular attribution may require evidence collection, verification, computation, auditing, dispute resolution, privacy safeguards, and continuing recalculation. Those activities have costs.

A simpler institution may sometimes perform better.

A conventional license may work when ownership is already clear. Profit sharing may avoid the need for granular causal estimates. Collective bargaining or a sector-wide royalty may provide compensation without examining every person's marginal contribution. A cooperative may deliberately choose collective rather than individual allocation.

PBS/MPS therefore should not assume that more detailed decomposition is inherently better. It has to earn its complexity.

The framework's own comparative standard is demanding: if another institution satisfies the relevant requirements more simply, cheaply, fairly, or reliably, the alternative should be preferred for that domain.

What Contribution Decomposition is ultimately trying to do

The underlying problem can therefore be stated quite simply.

Modern production can preserve the fact that many people participated while losing the relationship between their participation and the value that eventually emerged.

Contribution Decomposition is an attempt to keep that relationship open to evidence. It asks whether a documented contribution made a measurable difference; whether that difference depends on other contributions; whether its influence persists through time; whether the result remains stable under alternative models; and how much uncertainty should accompany any proposed allocation. The mathematical framework explores marginal contribution, interaction effects, persistence, Shapley-style allocation, and computational approximation as tools for answering those questions.

But the purpose is not to declare that every human contribution can be assigned a perfect number. In some cases the evidence may support relatively strong attribution. In others it may justify only a range, a coalition-level allocation, or a low-confidence estimate. And in still others, the responsible answer may be that individual decomposition should not be attempted at all. That refusal principle is consistent with the framework's emphasis on avoiding false precision and rejecting domains where attribution remains unstable.

That is why Contribution Decomposition matters to the broader PBS/MPS idea.

The Provenance Ledger can tell us who contributed what. The Performance Oracle can tell us what happened. Contribution Decomposition asks what relationship the evidence supports between the two.

Only after that analytical work comes the separate question of whether the resulting attribution should create an economic right, and if so, what kind.

That separation makes the proposal more modest than saying that provenance automatically creates ownership. It also makes the hypothesis more testable.

Contribution Decomposition succeeds only if its estimates prove sufficiently robust, interpretable, manipulation-resistant, privacy-compatible, and economically useful to justify relying on them. The broader PBS/MPS research accordingly treats operational and production viability as unproven and calls for bounded pilots, adversarial testing, comparison with simpler alternatives, and independent replication before stronger claims are made.

In short: provenance preserves the history of contribution. Contribution Decomposition asks what that history can responsibly tell us about value.

Three Distinct Questions

Provenance, performance, and attribution are not the same thing

Who contributed?

Provenance concerns the documented history of the asset and the identifiable contributions associated with it.

What happened?

Performance concerns independently observable outcomes associated with the documented asset.

How are they related?

Contribution Decomposition examines the relationship between documented contributions and measurable outcomes.

Conceptual Flow

How does Contribution Decomposition fit into PBS/MPS?

Step 1

Establish Provenance

Begin with documented evidence describing the asset, contributors, and contribution history.

Step 2

Observe Performance

Examine independently observable information describing relevant real-world outcomes.

Step 3

Examine Attribution

Analyze how documented contributions relate to the observed outcome within the applicable domain.

Important Boundary

Does Contribution Decomposition simply declare who deserves what?

No.

The proposed framework begins with evidence rather than an assumption that every participant contributed equally or that every documented contribution generated the same result.

Provenance establishes the historical record. Performance provides evidence about outcomes. Contribution Decomposition provides the analytical bridge between those two bodies of information.

The purpose is to make attribution capable of examination rather than leaving it entirely implicit.

Why does attribution become difficult in collaborative systems?

Contributions can differ in type, timing, scope, and relationship to a final outcome.

Some contributions may establish an original idea. Others may improve it, test it, implement it, document it, validate it, or contribute information that changes its eventual performance.

Contribution Decomposition is proposed as a framework for examining those differences without reducing the entire history of an asset to a single final owner or a simple record of participation.

Why is Contribution Decomposition relevant to AI?

Artificial-intelligence systems can incorporate knowledge, expertise, research, data, and creative material originating from many contributors.

PBS/MPS raises the policy question of whether systems can preserve meaningful relationships between documented human contributions and measurable downstream outcomes.

Contribution Decomposition is the analytical part of the framework intended to examine such relationships when attribution involves more than one contributor.

How does attribution relate to economic rights?

Analytical attribution and legally enforceable economic rights are separate questions.

Contribution Decomposition is proposed as a method for examining attributable contribution. It does not, by itself, create a property right, determine a securities classification, establish market price, or replace applicable contracts and law.

The larger PBS/MPS proposal explores how documented provenance, measurable performance, attribution, and lawful economic rights could operate together.

Regulatory Context

Was Contribution Decomposition part of the regulatory discussion?

ScarabCloud's September 2026 correspondence to Washington DFI identified “Contribution Decomposition & Royalty Flow” as the mathematical allocation component of the architecture submitted for review.

DFI subsequently explained that the broader PBS/MPS architecture extends beyond the laws administered by DFI and could raise legal issues including property rights and intellectual property.

DFI did not endorse or approve ScarabCloud's architecture or Contribution Decomposition methodology.

Formal Model

Is there a mathematical framework behind Contribution Decomposition?

Yes. ScarabCloud has developed a formal mathematical treatment supporting the Contribution Decomposition and Royalty Flow concept.

Meritocrat.org currently presents the public conceptual framework rather than reproducing the complete mathematical methodology on this page.

Technical documentation: Additional mathematical documentation may be made available separately where appropriate for research, legislative, regulatory, or technical review.

Why publish the concept without the complete formula?

Legislators, policymakers, researchers, and the public need to understand what problem the framework is intended to solve and how Contribution Decomposition relates to provenance and performance.

That public-policy purpose does not require publication of every implementation detail or mathematical parameter.

The public architecture therefore explains the function and logic of Contribution Decomposition while allowing detailed technical methodology to remain subject to separate review and publication decisions.
