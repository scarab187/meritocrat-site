DOCUMENT 1 OF 7

Executive Policy Summary — Washington Provenance‑Backed Securities Act

The Executive Policy Summary presents the policy case for preserving economically relevant contribution history in an economy increasingly shaped by intangible assets and artificial intelligence. It identifies an evidentiary and institutional gap: existing systems preserve title, contracts, payments, and recognized intellectual property more reliably than they preserve the underlying history of contribution. The document cites the shift from 83% tangible assets in 1975 to approximately 92% intangible assets in S&P 500 market capitalization by the end of 2025 as evidence of the scale of this economic transformation.

The Problem — The Ownership and Evidence Gap

The Summary does not argue that all intangible value is unowned or that every contribution creates a property right. Instead, it asks what happens when economically material contribution histories disappear before institutions can determine whether recognition, licensing, compensation, ownership, or no additional consequence should follow. Artificial intelligence makes that question more urgent because human judgment, data, examples, corrections, and operational knowledge can be aggregated and reused after their original contribution histories become difficult to reconstruct.

The Framework — Evidence Before Entitlement

PBS/MPS is organized around a disciplined analytical sequence: Provenance → Performance → Attribution/Allocation → Entitlement/Payment. The Provenance Ledger preserves relevant contribution evidence; the Performance Oracle supplies domain-appropriate evidence of observed outcomes; and Contribution Decomposition & Royalty Flow estimates attributable contribution under uncertainty and connects an approved allocation to economic participation only where an independent legal or contractual entitlement exists. An Exchange Protocol may support listing, pricing, transfer, settlement, reporting, and auditability when a legally transferable interest exists, but it is separate from the core analytical architecture.

SeedBid — A Bounded Agricultural Test

The Summary identifies SeedBid as the first specified domain application and proposed pilot, focused on agricultural genetic assets. Agriculture is presented as a useful testing environment because genetic lineage can be documented, agronomic outcomes can be observed, and existing plant-variety, patent, licensing, and contractual structures provide meaningful comparators. The proposed pilot would begin in shadow mode and test provenance integrity, performance measurement, attribution robustness, privacy, manipulation resistance, administrative cost, legal operability, and net participant value before broader economic consequences are authorized.

Why Washington

Washington is presented as a strong jurisdiction for a bounded PBS/MPS evaluation because it combines technology, agriculture, research institutions, securities regulation, privacy-policy experience, and a workforce increasingly affected by intangible and machine-mediated production. The immediate opportunity is not to declare a new asset class proven, but to create a disciplined process for study, stakeholder consultation, limited demonstrations, independent evaluation, and legislative review.

The Legislative Ask — Test Before Expanding

The Summary calls for a legislative work session, narrowly tailored pilot-enabling legislation, defined participant safeguards and evaluation requirements, and a formal report back to the Legislature. Broader authorization would follow only if evidence demonstrates reliable provenance, valid performance measurement, robust attribution, protected participant rights, legal operability, acceptable administrative cost, and meaningful performance relative to simpler alternatives.

Core principle: A contribution record is evidence, not an automatic ownership claim. PBS/MPS is designed to preserve enough credible history for law, contract, governance, and markets to determine what consequence, if any, should follow.

DOCUMENT 2 OF 7

Budget & Legal Justification — Washington Provenance‑Backed Securities Act

This document provides a preliminary fiscal framework and legal‑issue analysis for the proposed Washington Provenance‑Backed Securities Act. Rather than presenting fiscal self‑sufficiency, market adoption, or legal validity as established outcomes, it identifies the assumptions, authorities, constraints, classification questions, and institutional alternatives that Washington would need to evaluate before authorizing consequential implementation.

Evidence Before Fiscal or Legal Conclusions

The document expressly treats dollar amounts, staffing levels, adoption rates, revenue projections, legal classifications, and implementation timelines as assumptions or proposals unless independently verified. Its governing principle is that provenance is evidence, not automatic ownership; performance is not attribution; attribution is not entitlement; and payment requires an independent legal warrant.

Part I — Budget & Fiscal Analysis

The fiscal analysis retains an original independent‑commission model as an upper‑bound scenario, not as an appropriation request. That scenario estimates a Year 1 budget of approximately $5.64 million and a five‑year implementation cost of approximately $26.33 million. The document recommends that the Legislature compare less costly institutional alternatives, including a DFI‑led study, interagency structure, university‑supported pilot, and a dedicated commission, before selecting a permanent administrative model.

Modeled Revenue, Not Promised Revenue

Potential transaction, registration, licensing, examination, and sandbox fees are presented as policy options rather than validated revenue sources. Under the retained moderate scenario, modeled annual revenue exceeds modeled annual operating cost in Year 3 and modeled cumulative revenue exceeds cumulative cost by the end of Year 4. The document explicitly cautions that these results depend on assumed participation, transaction volume, fee authority, collection rates, and program costs and therefore should not be treated as forecasts.

Fiscal Discipline

Any public appropriation should be staged, capped, and tied to independently reviewed milestones and stopping conditions. No fee should be imposed before statutory authority, cost allocation, market participation, and distributional effects are established.

Part II — Legal Justification & Constraints

The legal analysis examines Washington's police powers, existing securities authority under RCW 21.20, federal‑state coordination, Commerce Clause and preemption questions, intellectual‑property constraints, privacy and data governance, and the legal requirements of any regulatory sandbox. Its conclusion is not that every PBS implementation is already authorized: classification and legal effect depend on the actual instrument, parties, governance structure, economic substance, and applicable state and federal law.

Securities Classification Is Instrument‑Specific

The document analyzes the federal Howey framework together with Washington securities law and concludes that some PBS implementations may qualify as securities while others may take the form of contractual royalties, licenses, cooperative interests, statutory payment arrangements, or other recognized structures. The label “PBS” alone does not determine legal classification.

Recommended Legislative Path

The recommended first step is a rights‑protective, nonpresumptive study and bounded pilot. The pilot should keep provenance, performance, attribution, entitlement, and payment analytically separate; compare PBS/MPS with simpler alternatives; measure all‑in administrative cost; disclose uncertainty; protect contributor privacy and due process; and stop or narrow the experiment if legal operability, manipulation resistance, or positive net institutional value cannot be demonstrated.

DOCUMENT 3 OF 7

Washington Provenance‑Backed Securities Act — State Bill

This document contains the full legislative text of the Washington Provenance‑Backed Securities Act (WPBSA), drafted for introduction in the 2027 Regular Session. It establishes the legal, regulatory, and institutional framework for provenance‑backed securities in Washington State. As stated in the bill’s findings, intangible assets now constitute “approximately ninety‑two percent of the market capitalization” of S&P 500 companies, yet lack standardized markets or valuation mechanisms.

Chapter 1 — General Provisions

The Act defines its purpose: to create a comprehensive regulatory structure for provenance‑backed securities (PBS). It establishes key definitions including provenance‑backed security, provenance ledger, performance oracle, PBS exchange, human capital provenance portfolio, and AI training royalty. The bill emphasizes Washington’s unique advantages — a world‑leading tech sector, major agricultural output, premier research universities, and a no‑income‑tax environment — as justification for first‑mover legislation.

Chapter 2 — Washington Provenance‑Backed Securities Commission

The Act creates the Washington Provenance‑Backed Securities Commission (WPBSC) as an independent regulatory body. The Commission consists of seven members appointed by the Governor, with expertise spanning securities law, blockchain, agriculture, labor economics, consumer protection, and intellectual property. The Commission is granted rulemaking authority, enforcement powers, oversight of PBS issuers and exchanges, and responsibility for administering the regulatory sandbox.

The bill appropriates $15 million for initial establishment and creates the Provenance‑Backed Securities Regulatory Fund. The Legislature expresses intent that the Commission become self‑sustaining within five years.

Chapter 3 — Registration & Regulation of PBS

This chapter establishes registration requirements for PBS issuers, PBS exchanges, and performance oracles. It mandates detailed provenance documentation, performance data, valuation methodology disclosures, and risk statements. The bill includes anti‑fraud provisions prohibiting falsification of provenance data, oracle manipulation, or deceptive practices.

Investor protection measures include suitability requirements, accreditation standards, and a 72‑hour cooling‑off period for rescission. The Commission is authorized to set fee schedules for registration, examinations, oracle certification, and sandbox participation.

Chapter 4 — Regulatory Sandbox

The Act establishes a Regulatory Sandbox Program enabling controlled testing of PBS innovations under modified regulatory requirements. Sandbox participation is capped at 25 entities (expandable to 50), with a maximum duration of 24 months plus a possible 12‑month extension. Transaction limits, investor caps, and enhanced reporting requirements ensure consumer protection.

As stated in the bill, the sandbox exists to “foster responsible innovation, gather empirical data to inform rulemaking, reduce barriers to entry, and protect consumers and investors throughout the innovation process.”

DOCUMENT 4 OF 7

National Provenance‑Backed Securities Framework Act — Federal Bill

This document contains the full draft of the National Provenance‑Backed Securities Framework Act of 2027, introduced in Congress to establish a unified Federal regulatory structure for provenance‑backed securities (PBS). As the bill states, intangible assets represent “approximately 90 percent of the total market capitalization of the S&P 500” and more than “$74 trillion in global asset value,” yet no Federal framework exists to regulate tokenized, performance‑verified intangible assets.

Title I — Findings, Purposes, and Definitions

Congress identifies the national regulatory gap: existing Federal securities laws do not address tokenized intangible assets with verifiable provenance and performance data. The bill cites workforce displacement — “92 million jobs… displaced globally by artificial intelligence” — and the extraction of worker expertise without compensation as key drivers for Federal action. The Act’s purposes include establishing a national PBS framework, protecting worker ownership rights, coordinating with states, and positioning the U.S. as the global leader in PBS regulation.

Title II — Federal Provenance‑Backed Securities Office (FPBSO)

The Act creates the Federal Provenance‑Backed Securities Office within the Securities and Exchange Commission. The FPBSO becomes the primary Federal regulator for PBS markets, responsible for rulemaking, registration, examinations, enforcement, and coordination with state regulators, the CFTC, and FinCEN. The Director must have “not fewer than 10 years” of relevant regulatory or technical experience.

The bill authorizes appropriations of $50M (2028), $75M (2029), and $100M (2030) to establish the FPBSO, with fee offsets from PBS market participants.

Title III — National PBS Registration & Regulation

PBS are formally recognized as securities under amendments to the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. The Act establishes Federal registration requirements for PBS exchanges and PBS issuers, including provenance documentation, performance data, valuation methodology, and risk disclosures. The bill mandates quarterly and annual reporting, independent audits, and strict anti‑fraud provisions prohibiting falsified provenance or manipulated oracle data.

Title IV — Worker Expertise Ownership & AI Training Royalties

This title establishes the first Federal ownership rights for workers whose expertise is used to train AI systems. As the bill states, every worker has “a recognized, enforceable ownership interest in their provenance‑documented expertise,” and such rights are non‑waivable. The Act creates a Federal framework for AI training royalties and mandates national standards for human capital provenance portfolios.

Titles V–X — Agriculture, State Coordination, International Standards, R&D, and Enforcement

The remaining titles establish agricultural PBS provisions, define the relationship between Federal and State PBS regulation, create international coordination mechanisms, fund research and development in oracle and provenance systems, and outline enforcement authority and penalties — including enhanced penalties for provenance fraud.

Economic Impact Report — Provenance‑Backed Securities & the Washington Economy

This report examines the economic case for Provenance-Backed Securities (PBS/MPS) in Washington State, focusing on intangible-asset markets, employment, GDP and revenue effects, sector-specific applications, and the evidence required before broader economic claims are justified. Rather than treating projected market activity as realized impact, the report proposes a bounded comparative demonstration to determine whether provenance, performance measurement, contribution attribution, and lawful economic participation create more value than they cost.

Headline Finding

The report identifies a large economic context for testing PBS/MPS: global corporate intangible assets approach $97.6 trillion, intangible assets account for roughly 92% of S&P 500 market capitalization, and tokenized real-world assets are projected to reach $18.9 trillion by 2033. But these figures do not establish a realizable PBS market. The report's central economic test is whether a bounded Washington demonstration produces measurable incremental value relative to the strongest realistic alternative after accounting for the full costs of verification, attribution, privacy, compliance, disputes, governance, and administration.

Section 1 — Global Intangible Asset Opportunity

The report describes a long-run "economic inversion" in which intangible assets rose from 17% of S&P 500 market value in 1975 to approximately 92% in 2025. It places that shift alongside an estimated $97.6 trillion in global corporate intangible assets and the projected growth of tokenized real-world assets. PBS/MPS addresses a different question from conventional tokenization: whether economically material contribution histories involving intellectual property, expertise, research, data, creative work, and other intangible inputs can be preserved and evaluated well enough to improve real economic decisions.

Section 2 — Washington Economic Baseline

Washington provides a substantial real-world environment for comparative testing. The report cites $717.5 billion in real GDP, a $159.7 billion information sector, $98.9 billion in professional and business services GDP, approximately $14.0 billion in agricultural production, and $1.69 billion in University of Washington R&D expenditures. These conditions do not prove PBS/MPS will succeed, but they provide technology, agriculture, research, professional expertise, and measurable economic activity against which a bounded demonstration can be evaluated.

Section 3 — From Projections to Demonstrable Economic Value

The report replaces transaction-volume-first forecasting with a comparative economic test: BP > CP. Measurable benefits from preserving and using contribution information must exceed the full institutional costs of recording, verification, attribution, correction, governance, privacy protection, dispute resolution, compliance, and administration. Evaluation should distinguish transferred activity, efficiency gains, unlocked activity, and new productive activity. Gross PBS transaction volume alone is not treated as new Washington economic output.

Section 4 — Employment and Workforce Measurement

Employment effects are treated as outcomes to be measured rather than assumed. The report distinguishes administrative employment, transferred activity, efficiency-related labor effects, and genuinely incremental market employment. A credible pilot should measure incremental FTEs, labor income, Washington residency, occupational composition, duration, substitution, training needs, administrative labor, and differences from the comparator. Statewide job projections should follow observed operating evidence rather than precede it.

Section 5 — GDP, Revenue, and Scenario Discipline

GDP and state revenue are treated as downstream empirical outcomes, not as premises for authorizing PBS/MPS. Earlier revenue figures are retained only as scenario outputs, not forecasts. For example, modeled Year-10 annual state revenue ranges from $48 million in the Conservative case to $187 million in the Moderate case and $420 million in the Aggressive case. The report expressly requires those scenarios to be recalibrated using observed participation, authorized fees, administrative costs, transaction activity, and incremental economic effects before they support broader fiscal claims.

Section 6 — Sector-by-Sector Testing

The report evaluates eight candidate domains: seed genetics, creative works, patents, personal data, scientific research, manufacturing processes, human capital, and biopharmaceuticals. Agriculture is prioritized for initial testing because genetic lineage can be documented, agronomic performance can be observed, and existing intellectual property and licensing systems provide meaningful comparators. Every later domain requires its own suitability analysis, participant safeguards, legal basis, comparator, measurement framework, and stopping rule. Success in agriculture would not by itself establish PBS/MPS viability in another sector.

Technical Appendix — Three-Layer Architecture and Implementation Roadmap

The Technical Appendix provides the detailed architecture, protocol specifications, data schemas, implementation roadmap, security controls, and compliance framework for Provenance-Backed Securities (PBS). Version 2.1 defines the core PBS/MPS analytical architecture as the Provenance Ledger, Performance Oracle, and Contribution Decomposition & Royalty Flow.

The governing analytical sequence is: Provenance → Performance → Attribution/Allocation → Entitlement/Payment . Each transition requires its own evidentiary, methodological, or legal warrant. The Technical Appendix therefore separates evidence, performance measurement, attribution, allocation, legal entitlement, payment, and market infrastructure rather than treating them as a single automatic process.

CORE DESIGN PRINCIPLE

Compliance, Auditability, and Evidentiary Discipline

The Technical Appendix states that PBS is designed for compliance with existing securities law, not circumvention . Its technical design emphasizes auditability, regulatory transparency, and investor protection.

Just as importantly, the architecture does not treat technical documentation as a shortcut to legal rights. Provenance does not automatically become ownership, performance does not automatically become attribution, and an attribution or allocation does not itself create a royalty, security, property right, license, or payment obligation.

Core PBS/MPS Analytical Architecture

The architecture separates three analytical functions. The Provenance Ledger establishes the evidentiary record. The Performance Oracle supplies observable, domain-appropriate performance evidence. Contribution Decomposition & Royalty Flow examines attributable contribution, interaction, persistence, uncertainty, and allocation, then supports economic participation only where an independent legal or contractual entitlement exists.

Layer 1 — Provenance Ledger

The Provenance Ledger is the evidentiary foundation of the architecture. It is designed to maintain an auditable record of creation, authorship, contribution history, custody, claims, transfers, and associated interests or restrictions.

The appendix specifies a consortium-ledger model with permissioned write access and public auditability, together with cryptographic evidence hashes, structured contribution records, identity verification, and append-only correction history. It evaluates Hyperledger Fabric, Hyperledger Besu, R3 Corda, and Avalanche Subnets, identifying Hyperledger Besu as the recommended candidate in the specified architecture.

Ledger outputs include Provenance Depth (Pd) and Provenance Reliability (Pr), intended to describe the depth and evidentiary reliability of the recorded provenance chain.

Explore the Provenance Ledger →

Layer 2 — Performance Oracle

The Performance Oracle supplies empirical outcome evidence associated with a documented asset or contribution set. Its role is to answer what observable performance occurred, under what conditions, and how reliably that performance can be measured.

The proposed architecture uses domain-specific data sources, standardized ingestion, validation and cleaning, independent oracle processing, cross-source comparison, and consensus publication. The appendix defines Output Yield (Oy) and Output Consistency (Oc) as principal performance outputs while recognizing that measurement methods and benchmarks must remain domain-specific.

Agriculture, creative works, patents, personal data, scientific research, manufacturing processes, human capital, and biopharma are treated as distinct measurement environments rather than as domains to which a single universal performance metric can be applied.

Explore the Performance Oracle →

Layer 3 — Contribution Decomposition & Royalty Flow

Contribution Decomposition addresses the analytical problem that remains after provenance and performance have been established: how documented contributions relate to an observed outcome, under what assumptions, and with what uncertainty.

The appendix examines marginal contribution, counterfactual estimation, temporal persistence, interaction and synergy effects, joint creation, and Shapley-based allocation where the specified value function and institutional objective make that framework appropriate. It also requires sensitivity and uncertainty to be disclosed when reasonable models produce materially different allocations.

The canonical output is an auditable attribution-and-allocation record, not automatically a token or exchange listing. That record may include the contribution set, performance evidence, value function, baseline, attribution methodology, interaction and persistence effects, allocation estimate or range, uncertainty, disputes, and the applicable entitlement rule if one exists.

Royalty Flow executes an entitlement; it does not manufacture one. A contract, statute, judgment, regulatory rule, governance instrument, or other valid legal basis must independently authorize any resulting payment or economic participation.

SEPARATE MARKET INFRASTRUCTURE

Exchange Protocol

The Exchange Protocol is part of the broader PBS framework, but it is not Layer 3 of the analytical architecture. It applies only where a legally recognized economic interest is transferable and market infrastructure is appropriate.

In those implementations, the Exchange Protocol may support listing, pricing, transfer, settlement, custody or control, reporting, compliance, market integrity, interoperability, and auditability. Market demand and liquidity may inform market-facing valuation, but neither market price nor liquidity establishes contribution, attribution, ownership, or entitlement.

PBS/MPS does not require every implementation to terminate in a secondary market. An implementation may instead terminate in a contractual royalty, license, revenue-sharing arrangement, cooperative interest, statutory entitlement, or another legally recognized payment mechanism.

Explore the Exchange Protocol →

Identity, Privacy, and Contributor Rights

The appendix describes an identity framework using W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials, with differentiated verification requirements for individual contributors, institutional issuers, and exchange or oracle operators.

The current architecture also places procedural protections alongside the technical system. Where a contributor's record becomes consequential, the framework calls for mechanisms addressing notice, access, correction, methodological contestation, uncertainty disclosure, privacy and purpose limitation, independent review or appeal, prospective exit where participation is voluntary, and protection against retaliation.

ARCHITECTURAL BOUNDARY

The Framework Includes a Refusal Rule

Granular contribution decomposition should be refused or replaced with a simpler mechanism when provenance evidence is inadequate, meaningful performance cannot be observed, attribution remains materially unstable, joint creation cannot be decomposed without false precision, privacy or surveillance costs are disproportionate, contributors cannot meaningfully contest consequential records, administrative cost consumes the distributable value, manipulation cannot be bounded, or no valid entitlement connects attribution to an economic consequence.

SeedBid™ — Agricultural Implementation

SeedBid™ provides the appendix's principal domain-specific implementation case. Its agricultural schema records elements such as crop species, variety or line designation, parent-line references, crossing records, selection history, existing plant-variety or patent references, trait information, and linked trial data. The architecture connects this provenance evidence with measurable agronomic performance information before any contribution-attribution or economic-participation determination is made.

Implementation, Validation, and Security

Beyond the three analytical layers and market infrastructure, the Technical Appendix specifies an implementation roadmap together with cybersecurity, identity, audit, regulatory-technology, disaster-recovery, and business-continuity requirements.

The appendix also makes a critical evidence-status distinction: mathematical coherence or architectural specification is not the same as operational validation. Stronger claims require testing of provenance integrity, performance validity, attribution robustness, manipulation resistance, contributor correction and appeal, computational scalability, privacy compatibility, administrative cost, and comparative performance against credible simpler alternatives.

Continuing Technical Research

The Technical Appendix should be read as a detailed system specification and research architecture, not as evidence that PBS/MPS has already achieved production-scale validation. Continuing research focuses on testing the evidentiary, measurement, attribution, privacy, economic, legal, and operational assumptions required for consequential implementation.

THE MERITOCRATIC PARTY

A Movement for Contribution‑Based Ownership

The Meritocratic Party is built on a simple principle: those who create value should own the assets their contributions produce. In the 20th century, society built infrastructure to own physical things—deeds, titles, stock certificates. In the 21st century, the economy runs on intangible assets such as ideas, data, expertise, and intellectual property. Yet the people who create this value lack the ownership infrastructure to participate in it.

Provenance‑Backed Securities (PBS) provide that missing infrastructure—documenting who contributed, measuring how well those contributions perform, and enabling ownership to be traded on regulated exchanges. This is not redistribution. It is recognition. It is not left or right. It is forward.

THE FIVE PILLARS

Values of the Meritocratic Party

1. Contribution Earns Ownership

2. Empiricism Over Ideology

5. Transparency Is Non‑Negotiable

Founder, Meritocratic Party · Independent Researcher and Theoretic Economist · Mill Creek, Washington

Joseph Richard Harmon is the founder of ScarabCloud Inc. and the Meritocratic Party, and architect of the Meritocratic Provenance System (MPS) and Provenance-Backed Securities (PBS) framework. Based in Mill Creek, Washington, Harmon developed the project around a simple principle: people who create value should have a meaningful path to ownership in the assets their contributions help produce. The work brings together provenance, measurable performance, contribution attribution, market infrastructure, and public policy to explore new ownership systems for the intangible economy.
