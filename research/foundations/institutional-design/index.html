Foundation 7

From Principle to Institutional Design

A normative argument becomes institutionally meaningful only when it specifies what should be measured, what may be corrected, who may act, and how uncertainty and error constrain that action.

Scope: This page explains the transition from the MPS normative argument to proposed institutional design. It does not claim that a particular implementation has been legally adopted, empirically validated, or approved by a regulator.

The normative conclusion

The preceding foundations distinguish performance from morally attributable performance, identify the laundering of antecedent advantage through otherwise legitimate market processes, preserve market information and productive incentives, and recognize the limits of causal measurement.

Taken together, those premises support a limited conclusion: institutions may have reason to identify and reduce the influence of non-meritocratic inputs when doing so can be achieved with sufficient evidence, proportionate intervention, and protection for legitimate agency and exchange.

Correction is not outcome equalization

The proposed corrective target is not inequality by itself. The target is the portion of an outcome reasonably attributed to inputs that the framework treats as morally arbitrary. A large reward may remain justified when it reflects genuine effort, cultivated skill, creative judgment, risk-bearing, or productive innovation.

This distinction limits the institutional mandate. A provenance system should not suppress contribution merely because contribution produces unequal returns. It should instead ask whether the conditions that produced and rewarded the contribution were sufficiently fair for the resulting desert claim to be credible.

Institutional design requirements

Evidence before correction A corrective decision should rest on documented evidence and an explainable method rather than intuition, status, or accusation.
Proportionality The strength of an intervention should reflect the magnitude and reliability of the identified provenance distortion.
Incentive preservation Institutional design should preserve returns to genuine productive contribution and avoid treating all unequal outcomes as suspect.
Contestability Affected participants should be able to inspect material reasoning, challenge evidence, and seek correction of an erroneous assessment.
Privacy and minimization Provenance assessment should use no more personal or institutional information than is reasonably necessary for the stated purpose.
Revision under uncertainty Models, indexes, and decisions should remain open to recalibration when evidence, assumptions, or observed effects change.

Levels of intervention

The framework favors corrections at the level where a distortion is produced. When unequal access to education, professional networks, capital, or institutional opportunity generates the distortion, expanding access is generally more consistent with the theory than equalizing every resulting income or asset position after the fact.

Ex-post correction may still be considered when an upstream correction is unavailable or ineffective, but it requires a stronger explanation: the relevant non-meritocratic input, the evidence connecting it to the outcome, the uncertainty of the estimate, and the reason the selected remedy is proportionate.

Administrative restraint

Measurement capacity does not by itself create authority to intervene. Any operational system would require a valid legal basis, defined institutional responsibility, review procedures, data-governance controls, and remedies for error.

The framework therefore separates three questions that should not be collapsed: whether a provenance claim is normatively persuasive, whether it can be measured with sufficient reliability, and whether a particular institution has lawful authority to act on that measurement.

How a proposed institution should be evaluated

A provenance institution should be judged by more than its stated purpose. Evaluation should include measurement error, false attribution, disparate effects, administrative cost, privacy burden, strategic manipulation, effects on productive incentives, and the accessibility of review and appeal.

A design that cannot expose these effects to independent examination should remain a research proposal rather than an operational decision system.
