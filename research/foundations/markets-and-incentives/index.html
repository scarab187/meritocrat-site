Foundation 4

Markets and Incentives

A provenance framework does not have to choose between correcting non-meritocratic advantage and preserving markets. The MPS proposal treats decentralized prices, voluntary exchange, productive incentives, and the information carried through markets as constraints that provenance correction should preserve rather than replace.

Core idea: MPS is proposed as a market-embedded corrective framework. Its objective is not to centrally determine prices or equalize outcomes, but to examine and, where justified, correct non-meritocratic inputs while retaining rewards for genuine contribution.

Why preserve markets?

Markets coordinate activity among people who possess different information, preferences, resources, abilities, and opportunities. Prices provide a decentralized signal through which economic actors respond to scarcity, demand, and productive possibilities.

The MPS framework therefore does not treat the price system as an obstacle to meritocratic justice. It treats the informational function of prices as something an institutional correction must respect.

This distinction matters. A system designed to correct provenance by replacing decentralized economic decisions with centrally determined valuations would face a fundamentally different information problem from a system designed to examine the conditions under which people enter market competition.

The proposed target is the provenance of market inputs, not the abolition of market allocation.

What prices tell us

A market price can communicate economically important information. It can indicate what buyers are willing to pay, what sellers are willing to accept, where goods or skills are scarce, and where economic demand exists.

But a price does not, by itself, contain a complete causal history of the thing being priced.

A wage can reflect the current market value of a worker's productive capacity without separately identifying how much of that capacity arose from effort, education, inherited resources, social networks, geography, or institutional opportunity.

Market valuation asks what an input is worth in exchange. Provenance analysis asks how the capacity or asset being valued came to exist and which causal inputs are relevant to a claim of desert.

The knowledge problem

The comparative-theory framework treats the problem of dispersed economic knowledge as a serious constraint on institutional design. Individuals possess localized information about their circumstances, opportunities, preferences, and productive possibilities that cannot simply be assembled into a complete central decision system.

MPS therefore does not propose that a provenance authority determine what every product, worker, company, or investment should be worth. The proposed auditing function concerns patterns and causal inputs relevant to advantage rather than centralized direction of production and exchange.

Auditing versus allocation

A central distinction in the framework is the difference between verifying inputs and allocating resources.

Allocation

Determines where resources should go, what should be produced, or what prices and quantities should prevail.

Provenance auditing

Examines the causal inputs to an economic position or reward and estimates the role of meritocratic and non-meritocratic factors.

In the proposed framework, provenance auditing occupies the second category. Market participants would continue making decentralized choices about production, exchange, investment, employment, and consumption.

Why incentives matter

Productive incentives are not treated as a secondary consideration. Incentive Preservation is one of the four stated components of the Meritocratic Provenance System.

The central design constraint is that an intervention intended to make economic rewards more meritocratic should not simultaneously destroy the incentive to create the productive contribution that deserves the reward.

Correct non-meritocratic advantage without suppressing genuine productive contribution.

That distinction separates the framework's stated objective from a policy that simply compresses the distribution of outcomes.

High rewards can remain legitimate

The framework is not opposed to large economic rewards as such. A large return can remain consistent with the theory when it is tied to genuine contribution.

Innovation, cultivated skill, entrepreneurial judgment, creative work, productive effort, and investment in one's own capacities remain economically meaningful inputs.

The question MPS adds is whether a reward attributed to those inputs also contains a component produced by antecedent advantages that cannot be attributed to the recipient in the same way.

The proposed incentive argument

The MPS foundations argue that distinguishing meritocratic contribution from non-meritocratic advantage could make reward signals more closely aligned with genuine productive contribution.

Under that argument, indiscriminate correction risks weakening the reward for both genuine contribution and arbitrary advantage. Provenance-sensitive correction instead attempts to distinguish between them.

What remains rewarded

Effort, cultivated skill, useful innovation, creativity, entrepreneurial judgment, and productive contribution.

What becomes visible

The estimated contribution of inherited, network, geographic, institutional, or other non-meritocratic advantages to the resulting economic position.

Voluntary exchange

The framework adopts a presumption in favor of informed voluntary exchange.

Voluntary transactions can create value for the parties involved, and the MPS proposal does not treat the existence of unequal outcomes from exchange as sufficient evidence that the exchange should be overridden.

At the same time, the voluntariness of a transaction does not answer every provenance question. Bargaining position, access to opportunities, productive capacity, and ownership entering an exchange may themselves have causal histories.

The framework therefore distinguishes the legitimacy of an exchange from the provenance of the positions the parties bring into it.

Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurial discovery is treated within the comparative framework as a potentially meritocratic input. Recognizing an opportunity, exercising judgment, creating a new product, and successfully organizing productive activity can ground a claim to reward.

Provenance analysis does not eliminate that claim. It asks whether access to the opportunity was also affected by factors such as capital resources, information networks, or institutional relationships that should be analytically distinguished from the entrepreneurial contribution itself.

Important distinction: examining the provenance of entrepreneurial opportunity is not the same as denying the productive value of entrepreneurial discovery.

Correcting inputs instead of setting outcomes

Where possible, the MPS proposal directs intervention toward the production of advantage rather than toward a predetermined final distribution.

The foundations describe access expansion in areas such as education, capital markets, and professional licensing, along with transparency and competition-oriented measures, as examples of the broader corrective approach.

The intended distinction is between changing the conditions of competition and instructing the market what final result it must produce.

Why this differs from simple tax-and-transfer

Conventional redistribution generally begins after market outcomes have occurred. Income or wealth is observed and then modified through taxation and transfers.

The proposed provenance approach begins with a different question: which inputs helped generate the outcome, and which of those inputs should count as meritocratic?

That difference is important for incentives because the framework's stated objective is not to impose the same correction on rewards regardless of their causal source.

Transaction costs

Provenance systems themselves are not costless.

Auditing requires information. Measurement requires analytical infrastructure. Advantage indexing requires models and assessment. Corrective institutions require administration.

Those costs form a genuine feasibility constraint. A provenance system cannot justify itself merely by identifying a normative problem. A deployable system would also need to justify the costs created by measurement and administration.

Institutional correction must be evaluated against its own costs.

The existence of provenance distortion does not establish that every proposed mechanism for measuring or correcting it is efficient.

Feedback and adaptation

Incentive effects cannot simply be assumed in advance. The MPS foundations therefore make ongoing assessment of behavioral effects part of Incentive Preservation.

Corrective mechanisms are intended to be adjusted when evidence shows that they create significant disincentive effects.

This makes incentive preservation an ongoing constraint on the system rather than a one-time design claim.

What remains to be established

The claim that provenance-sensitive correction can improve the alignment between reward and genuine contribution is a theoretical design argument.

Actual incentive effects would depend on institutional design, measurement quality, the form and scale of correction, participant behavior, and the economic environment in which the system operates.

The public foundations therefore should not treat favorable incentive effects as already demonstrated empirically.

What the framework does not propose

  • It does not propose replacing decentralized markets with comprehensive central planning.
  • It does not treat price signals as economically irrelevant.
  • It does not reject voluntary exchange merely because participants begin from unequal positions.
  • It does not treat large rewards as inherently illegitimate.
  • It does not propose suppressing returns to genuine effort, innovation, or productive contribution.
  • It does not assume that provenance auditing and corrective administration are costless.

The underlying design principle

Preserve the market's information function while improving the provenance of the inputs entering market competition.

In that formulation, markets and provenance correction are not opposites. Markets remain responsible for decentralized coordination and exchange. Provenance analysis adds a historical and causal inquiry into the conditions that produced the inputs being rewarded.

The success of the framework therefore depends on satisfying both objectives at once: reducing the influence of non-meritocratic advantage while preserving the information, freedom of exchange, and productive incentives that make decentralized markets useful.

Status: Design objective and theoretical argument. The MPS source materials identify Incentive Preservation as a core constraint, but favorable empirical incentive effects would require testing.
