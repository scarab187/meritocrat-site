Abstract

Markets preserve title, balances, transfers, and prices with exceptional precision, but contribution is a costly causal history. This paper asks when credible, portable contribution records improve coordination enough to justify recording, validation, attribution, correction, privacy, governance, and administration costs.

Markets record possession better than contribution because possession is usually cheaper to standardize, verify, and transfer. When the value of missing contribution history exceeds the cost of preserving it, institutional memory becomes productive infrastructure.

Why possession is cheaper to record

Possession is often a compact institutional state organized around a transfer. Contribution can be joint, delayed, context-dependent, and dependent on counterfactual inference.

The net institutional value test

Provenance is economically warranted only when expected reductions in information, verification, contracting, bargaining, dispute, and misallocation costs exceed the full cost of the institution.

Bargaining power and record control

Portable evidence can reduce dependence on whichever party controls the surviving organizational records. It can support a contributor’s claim, but it can also disprove an exaggerated claim.

Efficient memory, not maximal memory

The objective is selective, material, contestable memory. Domains should decline provenance where uncertainty, privacy burden, administration, gaming, or claim fragmentation exceed likely benefit.
