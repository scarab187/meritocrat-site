Evidence Before Entitlement

Provenance as an Institution of Economic Memory

The argument in one minute

Modern institutions accurately preserve title, contracts, possession, payment, and transfer, but often preserve the causal history of value creation incompletely. That gap is institutional amnesia.

Direct answer: Provenance should function as a governed record of who contributed what, when, under what conditions, through which dependencies, and with what observable connection to later outcomes. It improves the evidence used by law, markets, contracts, and theories of justice. It does not itself decide what anyone deserves, owns, or must be paid.
The unequivocal claimA society that preserves the provenance of value can evaluate claims of fairness and entitlement more honestly than a society that records final possession while leaving the history of creation obscure.

1. The problem: institutional amnesia

Economic institutions remember final holdings well. Land registries preserve title. Corporate ledgers preserve equity. Banks preserve payment. Contracts preserve agreed obligations. Courts preserve judgments.

These systems answer who presently holds the recognized right. They do not always preserve, with equal precision, how the value represented by that right came into existence. Collaborators, prior knowledge, institutional support, public investment, data sources, operational refinements, and cumulative dependencies can disappear from the surviving record.

The prior question: What actually happened in the creation, development, transfer, and capture of the value now represented by a holding?

Better evidence cannot eliminate moral or legal disagreement. It can prevent avoidable ignorance from posing as neutrality.

2. Categories that must not be collapsed

The framework depends on categorical separation. A record is evidence, not a verdict.

CategoryMeaning
ContributionAn identifiable input, act, judgment, resource, decision, expression, improvement, or sustained practice that may bear a causal relationship to an outcome.
ProvenanceThe traceable causal and transactional history of an asset, outcome, or contribution, including context, dependencies, transfers, and claims.
PerformanceObservable evidence concerning what an asset, system, or set of contributions does under specified conditions.
AttributionAn estimate of how documented inputs relate to an observed outcome, including marginal effects, interaction, persistence, substitution, and uncertainty.
DesertA normative judgment about what recognition, reward, burden, or standing is morally appropriate.
OwnershipA legally constituted bundle of powers, claims, privileges, immunities, duties, and limitations.
EntitlementA justified claim under the governing moral, legal, contractual, or institutional account.
AllocationA rule or decision assigning shares, responsibilities, recognition, or economic participation.
PaymentExecution of a recognized obligation through wages, royalties, licenses, distributions, damages, restitution, or another mechanism.
Key distinction: Contribution is not desert. Performance is not attribution. Attribution is not ownership. Ownership is not proof of moral desert. Documentation does not create a royalty unless a valid agreement, statute, judgment, or institutional rule supplies the entitlement.

3. The canonical PBS/MPS analytical sequence

1. ProvenanceWho contributed what, when, and in what context? What evidence and dependencies matter?
2. PerformanceWhat observable outcome occurred? Are the measures, baselines, and comparisons reliable?
3. Attribution & AllocationHow do documented inputs relate to outcomes, under uncertainty, and what allocation rule applies?
4. Entitlement & PaymentWhat independent rule makes the allocation enforceable, and through what remedy or instrument?

The sequence may stop at any stage. Provenance can be useful without a performance claim. Performance evidence can exist without reliable attribution. Attribution can be possible without recognized entitlement. Entitlement can exist without a tradeable security.

Design principle: Refusing to force every contribution record toward ownership is a feature, not a failure. Each transition requires an independent warrant.

4. What “economic memory” means

Provenance is not synonymous with blockchain, tokenization, or public disclosure. Those are possible implementations. The institutional function is to preserve relevant history in a form that legitimate decision-makers can examine, challenge, correct, and apply.

Identity and context

Connect a contribution to a verified person, group, institution, or lawful pseudonymous identity without unnecessary exposure.

Temporal order

Record when a contribution entered a process and how later developments depended on it.

Dependency

Preserve relationships among prior knowledge, common infrastructure, collaborative inputs, and downstream use.

Evidentiary integrity

Distinguish assertion, attestation, measurement, inference, dispute, correction, and final determination.

Contestability

Permit challenges to omission, misattribution, erroneous inclusion, or exaggerated causal significance.

Continuity

Allow later institutions to examine the same history after employment, control, technology, or market structure changes.

5. Standards for credible provenance evidence

Required safeguards

  • Relevance and purpose limitation
  • Verifiable sources, methods, dates, and limitations
  • Proportional burdens and consequences
  • Notice, access, correction, response, and appeal
  • Uncertainty disclosure and competing models
  • Domain-valid measures
  • Independent validation and adjudication
  • Correction of operative errors while preserving audit history
  • Publicly intelligible governance

What the system must resist

  • Collecting data merely because collection is possible
  • False precision in causal attribution
  • Institutional capture or conflicted adjudication
  • Surveillance, discrimination, credit scoring, or generalized human ranking
  • Turning an audit trail into immutable error
  • Using one domain’s metric in a different epistemic domain
Correction without ledger alteration: The audit history may preserve that an earlier claim existed, while the current record, decision interface, and downstream use carry the corrected or disputed status. Reversibility means that error must stop operating as truth. It does not require silent historical deletion.

6. The hard problem is causation

Establishing that an input existed is easier than establishing how much that input mattered. Productive activity is often cumulative, collaborative, path-dependent, and interactive. An indispensable contribution may create little value alone; a visible contribution may be easily substitutable; early infrastructure may define the possibility set for later work.

A serious attribution framework must test identifiability, materiality, persistence, interaction, substitution, and counterfactual plausibility. When those conditions are weak, the permissible consequence should weaken too.

Rule of restraint: Imperfect measurement is not an argument for blindness, but uncertainty is an argument against overclaiming.

7. Evidence in law: record first, decide under doctrine

Law already separates evidence from right. A receipt may prove payment without creating title. A laboratory record may support inventorship without resolving ownership. Employment records may establish participation while a contract assigns resulting rights elsewhere. A chain of custody may establish authenticity while legal consequence remains contested.

Provenance may assist questions of authorship, inventorship, consent, reliance, unjust enrichment, restitution, fiduciary obligation, chain of title, damages, licensing, or rectification. The legal consequence still depends on the governing doctrine, agreement, statute, remedy, and jurisdiction.

The legal boundaryEvidence opens the inquiry. Law supplies the rule of decision.

8. When provenance should be constrained or refused

A system should not be deployed merely because data can be collected. Constraint or refusal is appropriate when:

  • the contribution cannot be identified reliably;
  • performance evidence lacks a valid baseline or meaningful outcome measure;
  • attribution depends on speculative counterfactuals presented as precision;
  • privacy, autonomy, or security costs outweigh likely benefits;
  • participants cannot realistically contest the record or consequences;
  • measurement crowds out unmeasured but socially necessary activity;
  • a record becomes a prerequisite for dignity, housing, employment, benefits, citizenship, or equal standing;
  • administrative costs exceed the value of the information;
  • governance is controlled by parties whose claims require scrutiny; or
  • the proposed consequence exceeds the confidence and scope of the evidence.

9. Frequently asked questions

Does provenance automatically create ownership?

No. Provenance preserves evidence. Ownership requires a legally constituted right, and entitlement requires a separate moral, legal, contractual, regulatory, or institutional warrant.

Does every contribution deserve payment?

No. A contribution may be documented without reliable performance evidence, attribution, allocation, entitlement, or payment. The sequence may properly stop.

Is this a universal blockchain proposal?

No. Blockchain is one technological option. The paper’s claim concerns the institutional function of economic memory, not a mandatory technology.

Would better evidence settle questions of justice?

No. It can narrow factual disagreement and expose unsupported narratives. It cannot decide the moral weight of liberty, need, equality, agency, inheritance, public investment, or common ownership without further judgment.

Can a contributor challenge an attribution method without changing the ledger?

Yes. Methodological contestation concerns how recorded evidence is transformed into an attribution estimate. It allows challenge to the value function, baseline, comparison group, counterfactual, materiality threshold, interaction treatment, temporal assumptions, or allocation rule. It does not confer a right to rewrite historical events.

How can errors be corrected if audit history is preserved?

The system can preserve that an earlier claim existed while marking the current status as corrected, superseded, or disputed. Current decisions should use the operative corrected status, while restricted audit history preserves continuity.
