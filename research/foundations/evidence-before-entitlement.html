Abstract

Economic justice is often asked to decide too much from too little. This paper treats provenance as governed economic memory: evidence of who contributed what, when, under which conditions, and through which dependencies. It insists that provenance does not itself establish desert, ownership, entitlement, allocation, or payment.

A society that preserves the provenance of value can evaluate claims of fairness and entitlement more honestly than a society that records final possession while leaving the history of creation obscure.

The problem of institutional amnesia

Modern institutions preserve title, contract, payment, and transfer more faithfully than the causal history of value creation. The paper asks institutions to distinguish a legally operative ownership record from the fuller history needed to assess fairness.

Categories that must remain distinct

Contribution, provenance, performance, attribution, desert, ownership, entitlement, allocation, and payment are related but non-identical. A contribution record is evidence, not an automatic royalty or title.

The canonical sequence

The analysis proceeds from provenance to performance, then attribution and allocation, and only then to entitlement and payment under an independent legal, contractual, moral, regulatory, or governance warrant.

Evidence standards and boundaries

Credible provenance requires relevance, verifiability, proportionality, contestability, uncertainty disclosure, domain validity, independence, reversibility, purpose limitation, and accountable governance.
