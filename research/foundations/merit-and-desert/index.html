Foundation 2

Merit and Desert

Performance and desert are not the same claim. A person may perform well in a market without every cause of that performance being attributable to that person's own agency. The provenance framework asks what part of an outcome can reasonably ground a claim of merit.

Core distinction: A market can reward performance accurately while remaining silent about how the capacity to perform was produced.

The central problem

Meritocracy is often used to express two different ideas. The first is descriptive: better performance receives greater reward. The second is normative: greater reward is deserved because the performance is attributable to the person receiving it.

Those propositions are not equivalent.

A market price can respond to productivity without determining how much of that productivity arose from effort, cultivated skill, creativity, judgment, inherited resources, social connections, geographic circumstance, institutional position, or other causal inputs.

The Meritocratic Provenance System therefore treats the movement from performance to desert as a problem of causal attribution rather than assuming that market success itself proves merit.

Weak meritocracy

Weak meritocracy means that rewards track measured performance.

A more productive worker may receive a higher wage. A more successful innovation may receive a larger market return. A firm producing something buyers value more highly may earn more.

In this sense, meritocracy describes the relationship between observed performance and reward. It does not answer the historical question of how the person's productive capacity came to exist.

Weak meritocracy asks:

Did the reward respond to performance?

Strong meritocracy

Strong meritocracy adds a provenance condition. Reward should track performance that is, as far as practicable, attributable to the person's agency rather than merely to antecedent conditions the person did not produce or choose.

The framework identifies effort, cultivated skill, creative judgment, innovation, and genuine investment in one's capacities as examples of inputs capable of supporting a claim of meritocratic provenance.

By contrast, inherited resources, inherited network position, geographic circumstance, and institutional favoritism are treated as examples of inputs whose contribution to an outcome cannot simply be credited to the recipient's own agency.

Strong meritocracy asks:

How much of the rewarded performance is reasonably attributable to the person's own agency?

From performance to desert

The framework's reasoning can be expressed as a four-stage chain:

Performance
Provenance
Agency
Desert

Performance identifies an outcome or contribution. Provenance asks what causal inputs produced it. Agency asks which of those inputs can reasonably be attributed to the person. Desert concerns the normative claim that can be built on that attributable contribution.

Provenance is therefore the missing analytical step between observing performance and concluding that the resulting reward is deserved.

What happens to antecedent advantage?

The framework does not deny that inherited resources, networks, geography, institutions, or other background conditions can increase productivity. The relevant question is different: whether the beneficiary can claim those causal inputs as products of personal agency.

This distinction matters because an economic outcome can contain both meritocratic and non-meritocratic causal components at the same time. The framework therefore does not require classifying an entire person or an entire outcome as either deserved or undeserved.

Instead, provenance analysis asks whether the sources of an outcome can be distinguished well enough to separate genuine contribution from antecedent advantage.

Does meritocratic desert require equal outcomes?

No.

The framework does not identify inequality itself as the object of correction. Large differences in reward can remain compatible with the theory when those differences are attributable to genuine differences in contribution.

Its target is the portion of an outcome attributed to non-meritocratic provenance, not the mere fact that one person receives more than another.

What the framework preserves

Rewards for effort, cultivated skill, creativity, innovation, productive judgment, and other contributions attributable to agency.

What the framework questions

The automatic treatment of inherited, structural, geographic, network, or institutional advantage as if the resulting market return were wholly a product of individual merit.

What about talent?

Talent presents a difficult boundary case because productive capacity can reflect both natural endowment and deliberate cultivation.

The MPS position is not that differences in talent must disappear from economic rewards. The framework instead asks whether what is rewarded as talent represents cultivated capacity attributable to the person or whether the observed advantage substantially reflects antecedent conditions outside that person's agency.

This makes provenance analysis more demanding than simply observing who performed best. It requires attention to the causal history of the capacity that produced the performance.

Where this sits in political and economic theory

The broader research places the provenance framework in conversation with several established traditions rather than presenting it as a replacement for all of them.

  • Neoclassical marginal productivity theory: MPS accepts the importance of prices and productive incentives while questioning whether a market-clearing reward, by itself, establishes moral desert.
  • Rawlsian justice: the framework shares the concern that natural and social starting conditions are morally arbitrary, while seeking a more explicit institutional mechanism for provenance assessment.
  • Nozickian entitlement theory: MPS shares the emphasis on historical process, while extending the historical inquiry to the provenance of the capacities and advantages involved in acquisition and exchange.
  • Luck egalitarianism: the chosen-versus-unchosen distinction closely parallels the MPS distinction between meritocratic and non-meritocratic provenance.
  • Capabilities theory: the framework recognizes that resources and opportunities are converted into productive capacities under differing social and institutional conditions.
  • Austrian market-process theory: MPS treats decentralized prices and productive incentives as constraints to preserve rather than mechanisms to replace.

Interpretation boundaries

Several boundaries are important when interpreting the argument.

  • Market success is not treated as evidence that a person lacks merit.
  • Unequal rewards are not treated as inherently unjust.
  • Having an advantage does not by itself establish that an entire outcome is undeserved.
  • Provenance does not require perfect causal reconstruction.
  • The framework distinguishes analysis of structural patterns from a moral judgment about the worth of individual people.

These boundaries are important because the framework concerns the attribution of economic outcomes, not the attribution of personal worth.

The epistemic constraint

Strong meritocracy creates a measurement problem. Real economic outcomes have many interacting causes, and the counterfactual question is difficult: what would the outcome have been without a particular inherited, institutional, geographic, or network advantage?

The MPS foundations therefore do not require perfect provenance tracing. They instead propose probabilistic assessment and explicitly require uncertainty to constrain the strength of any resulting inference or correction.

Interpretation rule: Greater uncertainty about provenance should produce greater caution about claims of attribution. The framework does not treat an uncertain causal estimate as a certain judgment of desert.

The meritocratic claim

The central argument can be stated narrowly:

Reward tracking performance is not sufficient to establish meritocratic desert.

A stronger claim of desert requires examining the provenance of the performance and distinguishing, as reliably as practicable, the contribution attributable to agency from the contribution of morally arbitrary advantage.

On this account, provenance does not replace merit. It supplies the causal analysis required to determine what the word merit is actually referring to.
