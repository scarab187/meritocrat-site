Equal Performance and Meritocratic Desert

Equal measured performance does not necessarily imply equal meritocratic desert. MPS asks not only what outcome occurred, but what causal history produced the capacity responsible for that outcome.

Core distinction: Equal output establishes equivalence in measured performance. It does not, by itself, establish equivalence in meritocratic provenance.

1. Equal measured output can have different provenance

Two people can produce the same measurable economic result while the capacities producing that result have different causal histories. Productive capacity may reflect agency — including effort, cultivated skill, creativity, and judgment — while also reflecting antecedent conditions such as inherited resources, network position, geographic circumstance, or institutional advantage.

Weak meritocracy evaluates the measured performance. Strong meritocracy adds a provenance question: how much of that performance is attributable to the person's agency rather than to morally arbitrary advantage?

2. Rewards can contain both meritocratic and non-meritocratic components

The presence of antecedent advantage does not mean that a person's entire achievement is undeserved. Genuine effort, cultivated skill, creativity, judgment, and productive contribution can remain merit-relevant even when they coexist with advantages the person did not choose or produce.

MPS therefore treats provenance as potentially mixed. The relevant question is not simply whether a person succeeded, but which causal components of the resulting economic position are attributable to agency and which reflect non-meritocratic provenance.

3. MPS prefers upstream correction, but does not limit correction to the starting line

Where possible, MPS corrections operate on the processes that generate advantage. The framework emphasizes expanding access to the inputs of meritocratic success rather than simply equalizing outcomes after market processes have occurred.

This preference does not make downstream correction categorically unavailable. Where ex-ante correction is infeasible, the framework allows correction for the distorting effects of non-meritocratic inputs, calibrated to the estimated non-meritocratic component of the outcome.

4. Provenance correction seeks better attribution, not perfect causal purification

MPS does not claim that the complete causal history of an economic outcome can be reconstructed with certainty. Provenance assessment is probabilistic rather than perfect.

Where direct tracing is infeasible, the framework permits proxy indicators and requires the strength of correction to reflect the confidence of the underlying evidence. Uncertainty therefore limits the strength of an inference rather than disappearing from the analysis.
