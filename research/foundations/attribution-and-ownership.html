Attribution, Valuation, and Ownership

A contribution can be documented without being accurately valued, and it can be valued without automatically creating a legal ownership right.

The four-step logic

1. Record integrity

A provenance record can establish what data was submitted, by which verified identity, at what time, and with what referenced context. Cryptographic integrity can make later alteration detectable. It does not prove that the original assertion was true.

2. Factual attribution

Evidence and verification procedures must determine whether the recorded contributor actually made the claimed contribution and whether the contribution is distinct from prior work.

3. Causal and economic valuation

Performance evidence and decomposition methods estimate how the contribution affected an outcome and how that effect relates to value. Model choice, counterfactual design, interaction effects, persistence assumptions, and uncertainty can materially change the estimate.

4. Legal or contractual entitlement

A royalty, property interest, security, license, or other economic claim requires an applicable contract, statute, regulation, or binding legal determination. Attribution and valuation alone do not create that entitlement.

Non-equivalences

  • Timestamp ≠ authorship.
  • Authorship ≠ material contribution.
  • Material contribution ≠ unique causal value.
  • Causal value ≠ market price.
  • Market price ≠ moral desert.
  • Moral desert ≠ legal ownership.

Where PBS enters

PBS is proposed as an instrument layer that could connect documented contributions and performance evidence to economic claims. The legal character of any claim would depend on the actual instrument and governing law.

Boundary condition: The strongest public description is not that the architecture proves ownership, but that it proposes an evidentiary and analytical basis from which ownership or compensation rules could be constructed.
