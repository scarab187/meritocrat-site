Markets Need Memory

Human Contribution, Artificial Intelligence, and the Institutional Conditions of Legitimate Markets

The argument in one minute

Modern production is increasingly cumulative, intangible, distributed, and machine-mediated. Market institutions still need clear ownership, enforceable contracts, settlement, and legal finality, but those systems can preserve the final holder more reliably than the production history that created the value.

Direct answer: Legitimate markets may need governed institutions of economic memory that preserve relevant and contestable contribution evidence before society decides whether recognition, allocation, ownership, licensing, compensation, restitution, regulation, or no additional consequence should follow. Provenance does not itself establish desert or title.
The unequivocal claimLegitimate markets require more than memory of possession. Where cumulative and machine-mediated production makes contribution history materially relevant, society should develop governed means of preserving that history before deciding what rights or compensation, if any, should follow.

1. The structural mismatch

Value may be created by people, data, institutions, inherited knowledge, public infrastructure, organizational routines, software, capital, and machines. Ownership and compensation systems, by contrast, identify the final legal holder, contracting party, employer, assignee, or platform. That compression is useful for transfer and settlement, but can obscure production history that later matters to attribution, bargaining, licensing, liability, public funding, or rectification.

The public problem: The problem is not that markets recognize final rights. It is that final rights can become the only surviving account of how value was created.

2. Why artificial intelligence raises the stakes

Visibility can decline

AI may make upstream human contributions economically consequential while making contributors less visible. Annotations, corrections, heuristics, examples, and institutional supports can disappear into training pipelines or model updates.

Causal narratives become harder

Outputs may depend on combinations of inputs rather than single sources. Contributions can be redundant, complementary, architecture-dependent, or baseline-dependent, making simplistic creator-to-output stories unreliable.

Reconstruction can become impossible

Once source material is aggregated, filtered, weighted, encoded, and redeployed, reconstructing contribution history later may be technically difficult, institutionally costly, or impossible.

Scale magnifies error

A mistaken attribution or omission rule embedded in a large platform, procurement system, public program, or labor market can repeat the error at scale.

Important boundary: The fact that an upstream contribution mattered is not proof that it deserves payment. It is a reason to preserve evidence before the question becomes unanswerable.

3. The five-step public-interest argument

1. Cumulative creationModern value often emerges from dependency networks rather than isolated actors.
2. Terminal rightsRights systems identify holders needed for transfer, enforcement, settlement, and accountability.
3. Intermediate obscurityAI and intangible production separate contribution from later deployment and revenue.
4. Evidence can disappearFair attribution cannot be debated responsibly when participation, dependence, consent, transfer, performance, and use are no longer reconstructable.
5. Economic memoryPreserve relevant and contestable evidence first; decide consequence afterward.

4. Institutional memory and legitimate markets

Market legitimacy does not require agreement that every contributor deserves an economic share. The paper argues for distinguishable stages and shared evidence without shared doctrine.

ProvenanceRecords relevant history.
PerformanceSupplies observable outcome evidence.
Attribution / AllocationEstimates relationships and applies an explicit allocation rule.
Entitlement / PaymentRequires an independent warrant; payment executes it.

No stage automatically becomes the next. Property-rights, labor/desert, public-investment, legal, contractual, and other accounts can examine the same evidence and reach different conclusions, including that no remedy follows.

Markets need memory before they need a verdict: The immediate objective is not universal tokenization, a compulsory royalty, or a score of human worth. It is preserving evidence whose disappearance would predictably impair legitimate decision-making.

5. Risks of institutional amnesia

Bargaining power

When one party controls the surviving records of creation and use, that control can become an informational advantage in negotiation, mobility, licensing, and disputes.

Innovation policy

Weak production histories make it harder to identify which upstream inputs produce persistent downstream value and can encourage over-reward of visible terminal actors.

Disputes

Without evidence, disagreements about creation can harden into contests of power, reputation, or rhetoric rather than contestable factual questions.

Public trust

Legitimacy becomes harder to defend when institutions cannot explain, with evidence, how recognized allocations relate to cumulative production histories.

AI governance

Output-focused governance can omit whose documented contributions entered a system, on what terms, through which dependencies, with what authority, and with what later use.

6. What an institution of economic memory must do

RequirementInstitutional meaning
RelevancePreserve information material to a defined decision, not every observable fact.
VerifiabilityRecord source, date, method, status, and limitations.
ContestabilityProvide notice, access, correction, response, appeal, and adjudication.
UncertaintySeparate assertion, observation, inference, estimate, dispute, and determination.
Purpose limitationPrevent attribution records from silently becoming surveillance, credit scoring, employment exclusion, or compulsory human ranking.
Privacy and securityUse selective disclosure, minimization, retention limits, and proportionate access.
ReversibilityPreserve audit history without making error institutionally immutable.
IndependenceProtect measurement, validation, and adjudication from control by interested parties.
ProportionalityMatch recording burden and consequence to the value and reliability of evidence.
Domain validityUse methods appropriate to the field's epistemic structure.

The paper treats blockchain, conventional databases, registries, attestations, audit logs, contractual records, and privacy-preserving proofs as implementation options rather than mandatory technology.

7. What institutional memory must not become

Rejected uses

  • A universal score of personal worth.
  • A compulsory identity required for housing, employment, benefits, citizenship, or equal legal standing.
  • A presumption that documentation creates ownership or payment.
  • An immutable repository of uncorrectable allegations or model outputs.
  • A device for converting every social contribution into a market commodity.

Required restraint

  • Purpose limitation and privacy protection.
  • Meaningful correction and appeal.
  • Authority to decline attribution when evidence is weak.
  • Respect for courts, regulators, collective bargaining, and democratic judgment.
  • Refusal to measure domains lacking valid baselines or causal models.

8. PBS/MPS is a hypothesis, not a foregone conclusion

PBS/MPS is presented as one proposed architecture for provenance, performance evidence, contribution decomposition, and later economic participation. The larger public-interest claim does not depend on every PBS instrument succeeding or on tokenization.

Architecture clarification: A ledger is not ownership. A performance measure is not attribution. An attribution model is not exact causal truth. A Shapley allocation is conditional on the specified value function and adopted axioms. A royalty is not created by mathematics. The Exchange Protocol, where used, is surrounding market infrastructure rather than a substitute for the analytical sequence.

9. A humble research and governance program

Comparative analysis

Compare proposed provenance systems with contracts, registries, professional records, collective licensing, audit systems, courts, arbitration, and organizational governance.

Adversarial testing

Assume contributors and asset holders may strategically manipulate claims, metrics, baselines, attestations, use data, and validation.

Bounded pilots

Begin where contributions are identifiable, outcomes are observable, materiality thresholds are meaningful, privacy risks are manageable, and correction and appeal are feasible.

Independent replication

Require claims about attribution accuracy, transaction costs, mobility, licensing, innovation, and disputes to survive review independent of system designers and interested participants.

Explicit stopping rules

Stop or narrow pilots when error remains too high, contestability fails, cost exceeds benefit, privacy risk cannot be contained, gaming overwhelms metrics, or consequences exceed the evidence.

10. Frequently asked questions

Does every recorded contribution create a claim?

No. A record is not a claim, and a claim is not an entitlement. Better evidence can support rejection as well as recognition.

Does this proposal challenge clear ownership and legal finality?

No. The paper states that functioning markets need clear title, enforceable contracts, settled payment, and legal finality. Economic memory is bounded by materiality, proportionality, cost, and rules about when history is relevant.

Does AI prove that contributors deserve compensation?

No. AI raises the stakes because source histories can become economically important and harder to reconstruct. The consequence still requires an independent legal, contractual, moral, regulatory, or governance rule.

Is blockchain required?

No. The paper explicitly treats multiple technological and institutional implementations as options.

What if measurement is biased or gamed?

The paper proposes adversarial testing, multiple evidence streams, independent validation, uncertainty disclosure, correction rights, and authority to decline attribution.

Could provenance become surveillance?

Yes. The paper treats surveillance, compulsory identity, generalized human ranking, immutable error, and uncontrolled secondary use as reasons to constrain or reject a deployment.

Is PBS/MPS already validated?

No. The paper describes PBS/MPS as an author synthesis and normative public-interest proposal and calls for comparative, adversarial, bounded, independently replicable testing.

Claim-status note: This paper is an author synthesis and normative public-interest proposal. It does not claim that PBS/MPS is a deployed or independently validated production system, that comprehensive provenance is desirable, that attribution is universally measurable, that contribution evidence creates ownership, or that any particular technology is necessary. The proposed research posture is comparative, adversarial, bounded, independently replicable, and open to refusal where costs or risks exceed likely benefit.
