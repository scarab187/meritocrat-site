Research Foundation 1

Why Provenance?

Economic outcomes have causal histories. Provenance makes those histories analytically relevant instead of treating a current reward, price, or position as a complete explanation of itself.

Normative proposal Analytical framework Not legal doctrine No individual finding

Canonical definition

What provenance means here

Provenance means the traceable causal history of an economic outcome or contribution: the inputs that helped produce it and, where evidence permits, estimates of their relative effects.

An economic outcome may be a wage, profit, asset value, capital gain, opportunity, or other economic reward or position. Asking about provenance means asking what produced that outcome, not merely observing that the outcome exists.

Provenance therefore adds a historical and causal question to economic evaluation:

Which inputs produced the outcome, and which of those inputs are relevant to a claim of meritocratic desert?

Closed reasoning chain

Why the causal history matters

  1. An economic outcome may be produced by more than one causal input.
  2. Those inputs may include both agency-related contributions and antecedent conditions outside a person’s meaningful control.
  3. A market reward reports the value assigned through a market process, but it does not by itself report the complete causal history of the rewarded capacity or outcome.
  4. A claim of meritocratic desert depends on whether the rewarded outcome is attributable to morally relevant contribution rather than merely to antecedent advantage.
Inference Because a current reward does not by itself disclose the provenance mixture that produced it, the reward alone is insufficient to establish meritocratic desert.
Required conclusion

Evaluating the meritocratic legitimacy of an economic outcome requires information about both the observed outcome and its provenance.

Core distinction

Meritocratic and non-meritocratic provenance

Meritocratic provenance

The portion of an outcome causally associated, within this framework, with inputs morally attributable to agency, such as effort, cultivated skill, innovation, creativity, or judgment.

Non-meritocratic provenance

The portion of an outcome causally associated, within this framework, with antecedent conditions treated as morally arbitrary, such as inherited resources, network position, geographic circumstance, or institutional favoritism.

Interpretation boundaries

What follows, and what does not

Required

Conclusions supported by this page

  • Economic outcomes can have multiple causal inputs.
  • Current rewards do not, by themselves, reveal complete causal histories.
  • Meritocratic evaluation requires more information than market reward alone.
  • Provenance assessment may be probabilistic and incomplete.
  • Uncertainty must limit the strength of any conclusion.
Do not infer

Conclusions not supported by this page

  • All success is undeserved.
  • Every inequality is unjust.
  • Every advantage should be removed.
  • Markets or voluntary exchange are inherently illegitimate.
  • Any particular person’s reward has non-meritocratic provenance.
  • Provenance can be reconstructed perfectly.
  • The framework already creates legal rights or regulatory classifications.

Epistemic limits

This framework does not require perfect reconstruction

The proposed standard

Provenance analysis does not require a complete and certain reconstruction of every causal event. The proposed standard is sufficient reliability for the stated analytical or institutional purpose, with uncertainty reflected in the strength of the resulting inference.

The central limitation

The hardest question is counterfactual: what would the outcome have been without a particular contribution or antecedent advantage? This page does not solve that question for any individual case.

Required response when evidence is missing

If the evidence needed to establish a causal or normative conclusion is absent, the correct conclusion is: the proposition is not established.

Prohibited substitutions

  • Do not invent a missing causal link.
  • Do not convert a general example into a finding about a particular person.
  • Do not treat a proxy as direct proof.
  • Do not treat correlation as sufficient proof of individual causation.
  • Do not assign an Advantage Index from the contents of this page.
  • Do not hide uncertainty behind a precise-looking number.

Canonical answers

Questions this page answers directly

What does provenance mean in this framework?

Provenance means the traceable causal history of an economic outcome or contribution, including the inputs that helped produce it and, where evidence permits, estimates of their relative effects.

Does market success by itself establish meritocratic desert?

No. Under this framework, a market reward does not by itself disclose the complete causal history of the rewarded outcome or capacity. Provenance and background conditions must also be assessed.

Does the framework say all success is undeserved?

No. The framework distinguishes agency-related contribution, including effort, cultivated skill, creativity, innovation, and judgment, from antecedent advantage.

Does provenance require perfect causal reconstruction?

No. The proposed standard is sufficient reliability for the stated analytical or institutional purpose, with uncertainty made explicit.

Can this page determine how much of a particular outcome is non-meritocratic?

No. This page defines the framework but does not assess any particular person, organization, asset, wage, profit, or holding. A separate empirical provenance assessment would be required.

Status of this page

This page presents a normative and analytical definition used by the Meritocratic Provenance System research framework.

It does not report a completed provenance audit, establish the provenance of any particular economic outcome, create a legal entitlement, state an enacted policy, or claim adoption by a regulator, legislature, court, academic institution, or independent reviewer.

Research information only. Not legal, financial, investment, tax, securities, or valuation advice.
